# When AI Says It Did It: a verifiable action is not a convincing receipt
**Unpublished editorial sample — 2026-11-20. Not a publication, application, independent review, or proof of creator audience.**

An AI assistant reports that it changed a file. A log says the file was changed. A cryptographic signature says the log is authentic. Those are three different claims. None, by itself, establishes that the assistant performed the intended action.

Svyable's Control Evidence experiments supply a particularly sharp example. In a bounded local negative control, the observer authenticated the digest of a file that existed before the executor was ever started. The observer's statement about *what bytes were present* was correct. The inference that *the authorized executor created those bytes* was false. See [negative-control test](https://github.com/Svyable/Opps/blob/main/artifacts/control-evidence-2026/seed-recorder-envelope/test_preexisting_file_negative_control.py) and [ledger's recorded isolated result](https://github.com/Svyable/Opps/blob/main/LEDGER.md). This is a local demonstration, not a published third-party evaluation.

The distinction matters when AI systems are delegated tasks that have consequences outside the chat window. A status message is a claim by the actor; a signed observation is a claim by a measurement system. To attribute an action, an evaluator needs evidence about *when* the measurement occurred, what state preceded the action, which process had authority to act, and whether the observation channel was independent of the actor. If the executor can choose the observer's key, manufacture its event, or substitute the observed state, the signature protects the wrong boundary.

A subsequent Svyable experiment generated a fresh target and rejected preexisting matching bytes. Five isolated regression tests reportedly passed. This closes one easy false-attribution route, not the general problem. The prestate gate was not yet integrated into the two-process observer, and shared-host races and key custody remain unresolved. [Implementation](https://github.com/Svyable/Opps/blob/main/artifacts/control-evidence-2026/seed-recorder-envelope/fresh_action_gate.py) · [tests](https://github.com/Svyable/Opps/blob/main/artifacts/control-evidence-2026/seed-recorder-envelope/test_fresh_action_gate.py).

A useful evaluator would therefore distinguish at least four outcomes: **verified** (the required action was independently observed under defined trust assumptions), **rejected** (evidence contradicts the claim), **unknown** (evidence is absent or incomplete), and **not tested** (the measurement never happened). Collapsing unknown into success rewards convincing reports rather than completed work. Collapsing unknown into failure can also hide instrumentation defects. Both mistakes contaminate safety assessments.

This is not an argument that a file-write experiment proves anything about catastrophic AI risk. It does not. The research question is narrower and testable: can better action provenance change an evaluator's decision in adversarial agent tasks, compared with self-report and signed-log baselines? A credible study would preregister false-positive rates, test replay and preexisting-state attacks, separate recorder credentials from the actor, publish exact code revisions and negative results, and ask external evaluators whether the evidence changes their decisions. Until then, it is a promising measurement problem, not a deployed safeguard.
