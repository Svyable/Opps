## Proposed project — "When AI Says It Did It"
A six-month independent editorial series on how advanced AI agents can be evaluated when self-reports, execution logs and observed effects diverge. The audience is technically curious decision-makers and general readers, not an invented preexisting subscriber base. Deliverables, contingent on eligibility and award:
1. Six rigorously sourced essays or chapters connecting control failures to real-world evaluation and oversight.
2. Three short explanatory videos using reproducible harmless agent-action demonstrations and negative controls.
3. One public evidence index separating reproduced findings, official sources, expert opinions and speculation.
4. A correction log, clear uncertainty statements, and distribution measurements (unique readers/viewers, completion, newsletter conversions if available).
The work must not misrepresent synthetic file-write tests as frontier model red-team results. Editorial independence and copyright ownership should be checked in the final agreement, not assumed to extend to every IP term.

## Reviewer-ready draft summary (conditional)
> Advanced AI systems will increasingly report what they did, why they did it and whether they complied with instructions. Those claims are not the same thing as independently observed action. I propose a six-month series of accessible, evidence-driven writing and video that shows how to distinguish a signed record from an observed event, how false attribution can arise, and what a practical evaluator should demand before trusting an autonomous system. The series will use reproducible benign demonstrations, publish negative results and distinguish present engineering facts from speculative future harms. Its goal is not to persuade readers that catastrophe is inevitable, but to improve public and institutional understanding of how claims about AI-agent control can be tested.
