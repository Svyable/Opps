## Summary (38 words)

Reciprocal Evidence will prototype portable, independently checkable frontier-AI evaluation receipts for Canadian and Dutch institutions. Each receipt records what was tested, by whom, under which conditions, and what remains unknown, making safety evidence easier to scrutinize across borders.

## Description (application draft)

Middle powers are being asked to make consequential decisions about frontier AI using evidence they often cannot independently inspect. A developer's safety assertion, an evaluator's measured finding, and a finding reproduced by a second party should not arrive on a policymaker's desk looking identical.

I propose Reciprocal Evidence: a six-month public-interest pilot to make frontier-AI evaluation findings portable, inspectable, and harder to exaggerate as they move between institutions. The basic unit is an **evaluation receipt**—a structured record binding a claim to a specific model/version, test question, method, evaluator, independence disclosure, source artifacts, limitations, and outcome. Receipts distinguish a verified provenance chain from a verified safety claim. Missing evidence, failed controls, and disputed interpretations remain visible rather than being averaged away.

The test case is **Canada and the Netherlands**. These countries have different forms of influence over frontier AI: Canada's AI research and public-policy ecosystem and the Netherlands' position in the semiconductor supply chain and European regulatory environment. Both need ways to evaluate assurances from companies and counterparties they do not control. The research question is deliberately narrow: can a technically informed reviewer in one jurisdiction understand, challenge, and reproduce the evidentiary basis of an evaluation issued in the other without relying on an uncheckable summary?

The pilot would begin with public evaluation reports, model and system cards, documented agent-behavior findings, and permitted open-model tests. It would not assume privileged access to frontier developers, regulators, or confidential evaluation data. We would create an open receipt specification and reference validator; produce a small public corpus of positive, negative, and ambiguous examples; test whether independent reviewers can reproduce the declared checks; and interview or consult policy-domain specialists about the evidence needed for cross-border use. Recruited collaborators would be paid and disclosed. No institutional partnerships are currently secured.

**Six-month sequence:** months 1–2, map use cases and freeze the schema, threat model, and evaluation plan; months 3–4, implement receipts and provenance checks, construct a minimum twelve-case evidence corpus, and perform adversarial testing; months 5–6, commission external review where feasible, test cross-jurisdiction interpretability, and publish a practical adoption note for middle-power evaluators and policy teams. The larger nine-month version would fund two local advisory tracks, additional replication exercises, accessibility work, and a more substantial open reference implementation.

The outputs would be public, with clear rights and redaction rules. We would report what fails: disputed model identity, weak evaluator independence, absent source artifacts, irreproducible checks, incompatible jurisdictional definitions, or no credible adoption path. Success is not a new certification badge. It is a defensible account of what independent AI evaluations can—and cannot—prove to a government that did not conduct them.
